[5]Guaranteeing the Global Gateway: National DFIs and the Transformation of EU Development Finance
with Simone Dietrich
Journal of European Integration, Forthcoming
Abstract
The recent European Global Gateway Initiative pledged more than 300 billion euros in public and private investment in infrastructure to satisfy international demand while maximizing the economic interests of member countries. However, European development cooperation policy is traditionally far removed from the hard sectors, with an emphasis on promoting democracy and social well-being. How has the European development cooperation architecture evolved to support infrastructure investment in the Global South? We trace the way in which the European Commission has reformed the European development architecture to place national Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) center stage in the financing of the Global Gateway Initiative. Using an original database, we show that DFIs are at the core of the GGI initiative and that the projects funded support European economic interests.
[4] Populism and the Politics of Aid Classification: Evidence from the OECD Development Assistance Committee
with Simone Dietrich, Katharina Fleiner, and Alice Iannantuoni
International Studies Quarterly, Forthcoming
Abstract
Populist leaders increasingly criticize global governance systems, portraying them as elitist and threatening national sovereignty. While research has explored how populism affects international organizations, less is known about how a rise in populism in donor countries affects engagement with standards in international development cooperation. This research note argues that populism affects how donors classify and present foreign aid projects. Focusing on the OECD's Development Assistance Committee (DAC), we theorize a mechanism of classification avoidance, whereby bureaucrats anticipate political scrutiny from populists and reduce the use of policy markers that signal alignment with global priorities. Using project-level data on aid activities from 1998 to 2022, we show that increases in anti-elitism in donor legislatures are associated with declines in the share of aid classified as targeting DAC priority areas, alongside stable project screening practices and some but more limited sectoral reallocation.

Between Markets and Statecraft: Shareholder Capital and Bilateral Development Finance Institutions’ Investment
To be presented, APSA 2026
Abstract
Donor governments increasingly deliver development cooperation through bilateral development finance institutions (DFIs), several of which have opened their capital to commercial shareholders. What happens when private owners enter public development institutions mandated to invest and assume risk where private investors will not? I argue that ownership structure shapes a DFI's capacity to absorb risk: commercial shareholders impose financial discipline that narrows developmental additionality, but relax it where they hold a direct stake. Drawing on an original dataset of 11,180 investments by 12 donor-DFIs across 129 recipient countries (1970–2024), and a shift-share instrument, I show that commercially owned DFIs reduce commitments to recipients whose creditworthiness deteriorates, while fully public DFIs do not. This discipline lapses in sectors attractive to commercial shareholders. Preliminary findings drew from a work-in-progress original individual-level dataset on DFI board directors support the mechanism. The paper illuminate the role of development finance in pursuing commercial statecraft and underlines the conditions under which DFIs can fill the gap left by aid and global capital retrenchment.
What Geopolitical Returns Does ODA Bring?
with Simone Dietrich
Kiel Working Paper No. 2305, 2025
Abstract
We review the literature on the political economy of foreign aid, examining the geopolitical returns generated by Official Development Assistance (ODA). Our paper identifies under which conditions donors are able to influence political and economic outcomes in recipient countries, shape their behavior in global affairs, and adjust to domestic and international challenges. Building on an emerging body of research in international development finance, we propose future directions for the study of ODA in a contested global landscape.

Measuring Debt Transparency in Developing Countries: An IRT Model
with Zoe Ge, Layna Mosley, and B. Peter Rosendorff
Abstract
Debt transparency is essential for managing sovereign debt, but it is often hindered by political and technical constraints. This paper introduces the Princeton-NYU Debt Transparency (PNDT) measure, a standardized index of debt transparency over time and across countries. The PNDT leverages missing data from the World Bank's Debtor Reporting System using a Bayesian item response theory model. Covering 113 countries from 1994 to 2022, the index is correlated with the domestic electoral cycle, openness, financial crises, and other international political dynamics. Debt transparency is not merely a matter of technical capacity but is also a political choice made by elected officials.
